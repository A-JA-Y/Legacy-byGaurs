Every project within forty kilometres of Jewar is being sold to you on the back of the airport. That does not make the airport argument wrong — it makes it unremarkable. If the airport is the whole reason to buy, then any plot on the Yamuna Expressway will do, and you should buy the cheapest one.

The more useful question is narrower: **given the airport, what kind of asset in this corridor is actually worth holding?** This piece argues that the answer has less to do with proximity and more to do with supply.

---

## The Airport Thesis

<a id="airport" />

Noida International Airport at Jewar is the largest infrastructure event in the NCR's recent history. Airports reliably re-rate the land around them — the pattern is consistent enough across Indian metros to be treated as a base case rather than a hope.

But two things about that pattern are routinely misread by buyers:

<Callout type="warning">
  **Airport proximity lifts land, not necessarily your unit.** Land in the catchment re-rates. A specific apartment re-rates only if its supply is constrained. If ten thousand comparable units come to market in the same corridor over the same period, the airport premium is competed away — into the developers' launch prices, not your resale.
</Callout>

The second misread is timing. Airport-driven appreciation is not a launch-day event; it accrues over the operational ramp. That is a multi-year hold, which means the carrying characteristics of the asset — maintenance, liquidity, rentability, and whether the address is pleasant to actually live in — matter more than the headline catchment map.

## The Scarcity Argument

<a id="scarcity" />

Legacy by Gaurs is **265 condominiums and 20 villas**. That is the entire project. Not phase one — the whole thing.

Two features of that inventory matter more than they first appear.

**There is no small inventory.** Every condominium is a 4 BHK of at least 3,510 sq. ft. There is no studio tier, no 2 BHK tier, nothing that will later trade at a discount and drag the address's benchmark rate down with it. In mixed-inventory townships, the cheapest unit sets the price that portals quote and buyers anchor on. Here there isn't one.

**There is no room to add more.** The project is an infill inside a township that is already built. Gaurs cannot respond to demand by launching Phase 2 next door, because there is no next door. Compare this to a greenfield launch on the Yamuna Expressway, where the developer holds a land bank and every subsequent phase competes directly with the units it already sold you.

<PullQuote>
  Scarcity you can verify on a master plan is worth more than scarcity you are asked to take on faith.
</PullQuote>

## Delivered vs. Promised

<a id="delivered" />

The most underrated feature of this project is that the *township* is not a rendering.

Jaypee Greens exists. The 18-hole Greg Norman golf course exists and is played on. The Atlantic sports complex, the Boomerang Club, the Town Centre, the school, the arterial roads, the water and the power are all in place and have been for years. You can drive there this weekend and walk it.

What you are taking construction risk on is one project inside that township — not the township itself. That is a materially different risk profile from buying into a greenfield master plan where the clubhouse, the school and the road are all line items on a brochure.

## Risks Worth Naming

<a id="risks" />

A page that only lists reasons to buy is an advertisement, not an analysis. The honest risks:

<Callout type="warning">
  **Handover is a raw structure.** The brochure is explicit: units are handed over as a raw structure with façade development, and works not listed in the specifications fall within the buyer's scope. Your all-in cost is the unit price *plus* a fit-out you must budget for. Get the developer scope in writing before you model returns.
</Callout>
